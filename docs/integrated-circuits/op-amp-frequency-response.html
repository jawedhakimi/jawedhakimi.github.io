Op-amp frequency response

Integrated circuits

The gain formulas on the op-amp basics page — \(1 + R_f/R_{in}\), \(-R_f/R_{in}\) — assume an ideal op-amp with infinite, frequency-independent open-loop gain. Real ones don’t have that, and the way they fall short shapes both how much bandwidth you actually get and whether the circuit stays stable at all.

Open-loop gain isn’t flat

A real op-amp’s open-loop gain is enormous at DC (typically 100 dB to 120 dB — a ratio of 100,000 to 1,000,000) but starts rolling off at a surprisingly low frequency, often just single-digit to tens of Hz. That’s deliberate: op-amps are internally frequency-compensated with a dominant pole specifically to keep the response falling at a predictable, well-behaved −20 dB/decade past that corner, all the way out to where the gain reaches unity (0 dB).

Gain-bandwidth product

Past the dominant pole’s corner frequency, gain falls off as \(1/f\) — which means gain times frequency is constant. That constant is the gain-bandwidth product (GBW), a single number on every op-amp datasheet:

\[ GBW = A_{open-loop} \times f_{corner} = A_{closed-loop} \times f_{-3dB, closed-loop} \]

Both sides of that equation describe the same straight line on a log-log plot — one point on it is the open-loop DC gain and its corner frequency; every other point is some closed-loop gain and the bandwidth it gets. Rearranged into the form actually used when picking resistor values:

\[ f_{-3dB} \approx \frac{GBW}{A_{noise\ gain}} \]

“Noise gain” here means \(1 + R_f/R_{in}\) in both the inverting and non-inverting configurations — for the inverting amp specifically, that’s not the same as its signal gain (\(-R_f/R_{in}\)); the feedback network sets bandwidth according to the noise gain regardless of which input the signal actually enters.

Calculator: closed-loop bandwidth

More gain always costs bandwidth, in direct proportion — a 10 MHz GBW part gives roughly 1 MHz of bandwidth at a noise gain of 10, or 100 kHz at a noise gain of 100. If a design needs both high gain and wide bandwidth, that’s usually solved with multiple lower-gain stages in series rather than one stage doing it all, since bandwidth divides by the product of per-stage gains but each stage individually only pays for its own gain.

Phase lag and phase margin

Every pole in a system contributes phase lag that reaches 45° right at its corner frequency and approaches a full 90° well above it. A single dominant pole — most of an op-amp’s usable range — contributes phase approaching −90°. Real op-amps have additional (higher-frequency) poles from other internal stages, which add further phase lag as frequency climbs toward and past the unity-gain frequency.

This matters because of how the feedback loop actually behaves: if the total phase lag around the loop reaches a full 180° at the frequency where loop gain is still 1 (0 dB), the negative feedback the whole circuit depends on effectively becomes positive feedback — the circuit oscillates instead of amplifying.

Phase margin is the safety cushion against that: 180° minus the total phase lag, measured at the frequency where loop gain crosses 0 dB. A single dominant pole alone gives close to 90° of margin (comfortably stable); each additional pole below the crossover frequency eats into it. Common design targets:

  • ≥ 45° phase margin — generally considered the minimum for acceptable behavior.
  • ~60–65° phase margin — a common sweet spot: well damped, minimal step-response overshoot, without sacrificing more bandwidth than necessary.
  • < 45° — increasing peaking in the frequency response and overshoot/ringing in the step response; margins approaching 0° mean sustained oscillation.

Reading a Bode plot

The standard way this all gets presented: two stacked plots sharing a log-frequency x-axis. The magnitude plot (dB vs. frequency) shows straight-line segments — flat, then breaking downward by 20 dB/decade at each pole’s corner. The phase plot (degrees vs. frequency) shows each pole’s transition, passing through −45° at its own corner and flattening out near −90° roughly a decade above it. Reading both together at the loop’s unity-gain crossover point is exactly how phase margin gets checked, whether by hand from a datasheet graph or automatically in simulation.
